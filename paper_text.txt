Fraud Guard UPI: Stopping Financial Fraud 
Before the Money Moves 
A Real-Time AI Fraud Detection System for India’s UPI Ecosystem 
Divyansh Gahlaut,Harsh Sindhani Bhojwani 
 
Abstract 
Picture this. You get a call. The voice on the other end is your sister — same tone, same way she says your name, same slight 
accent she has had since childhood. She is in trouble. She needs ₹50,000 transferred over UPI, right now, please. You pause for 
maybe three seconds. Then you send it. Two hours later you are sitting with your actual sister, who never made any such call, and 
realising that the voice was generated by an AI stitched together from a three second clip on her Instagram. The money is gone. 
UPI transfers do not come back. 
This is not a made-up scenario. This exact thing — or some close version of it — happened to real people across India throughout 
2024. And its just one kind of fraud in an epidemic that produced over 95,000 complaints and erased roughly ₹1,750 crore in a 
single fiscal year. What bothers us more than the numbers is the design flaw hiding in plain sight: every fraud detection system 
running on bank servers today works the same way. It waits for the transaction to complete, notices something wrong, and then 
tries to help. For a payment system where reversals are nearly impossible, waiting is the same as failing. 
FraudGuard UPI is our attempt to fix the sequencing. Six machine learning models run the moment a payment is initiated — not 
after. They look at transaction behaviour, UPI ID reputation, recent SMS messages, call history, audio deepfakes, and the structure 
of the broader transaction network. A fraud decision lands in under 200ms. In testing, 91.3% of fraud attempts were stopped 
before a single rupee moved. 
Keywords: UPI fraud detection, deepfake voice, MuRIL, GraphSAGE, real-time ML inference, vishing, FastAPI, Quasar Framework 
 
 
 
 
1. INTRODUCTION 
India did not ease into digital payments. It jumped. January 
2025, UPI processed 13.89 billion transactions worth more 
than ₹21.41 lakh crore in one single month. A payment 
system that barely existed a decade back now moves more 
money than most country’s entire banking sectors handle 
in a year. It reached vegetable sellers, daily wage workers, 
small town shopkeepers — people that formal banking 
honestly never bothered with. That part is genuinely 
impressive. 
But the same openness that makes UPI work for everyone 
also makes it work for fraudsters. You don't need a hacked 
bank account or some fancy malware to steal through UPI. 
You just need to trick someone into tapping Pay. And 
people, especially when they are scared or in a hurry, are 
surprisingly easy to trick. 
The fraud methods keep evolving. Vishing calls from 
scammers pretending to be TRAI officers or bank officials. 
Fake QR codes swapped in at shops that route payments to 
criminal accounts. Collect requests designed to look like 
incoming payments when they are actually pulling money 
out. None of this is new. Fraudsters have been running 
these plays for years now. 
What is genuinely new — and honestly a bit frightening — 
is AI voice cloning. Today a fraudster can grab three seconds 
of someone’s voice from a public video and produce a clone 
convincing enough to fool that person’s own family on a 
phone call. In cases we looked at from 2024, victims 
transferred money within ten minutes of receiving these 
calls. The AI did not need to be perfect. It just needed to 
hold up for one short conversation under emotional 
pressure. 
Most detection systems were not built for this world. They 
sit on bank servers, analyse completed transactions, and 
flag problems after money has already gone. For a payment 
system where reversals are nearly impossible, that design is 
fundamentally broken. whole premise with FraudGuard UPI 
is different: catch it before the user taps Pay, or don't 
bother. 
1.1 What We Are Actually Solving 
The core question sounds simple enough: can a mobile app, 
using AI, stop a UPI fraud in the exact moment it’s 
happening — before the payment goes through — while 
staying fast enough that the user never even notices the 
delay? 
Inside that question are several harder problems that, to 
our knowledge, nobody has tackled together in one system: 
• How do you detect a cloned voice on a live phone call 
when audio quality is bad and you have maybe 200 
milliseconds to decide? 
• How do you classify a phishing SMS written in Hinglish 
— Hindi typed out in Roman script — when every 
standard English model falls apart on it? 
• How do you identify a money mule network when 
none of the individual accounts look suspicious by 
themselves? 
• How do you get a working fraud model running on day 
one, when there is no labelled historical data yet? 
• How do you collect behavioural signals from 
someones device without it becoming a surveillance 
product? 
1.2 What This Paper Adds 
• A six-model ML architecture designed specifically for 
UPI — not borrowed from some generic fraud dataset, 
but built around India’s actual payment landscape and 
its messy linguistic reality 
• A real-world evaluation of Resemble AI DETECT-2B for 
deepfake voice detection inside a live mobile fraud 
workflow 
• A novel Call-to-Transaction Correlation algorithm that 
connects suspicious incoming calls to UPI payments 
that follow shortly after 
• A complete technical blueprint — from the frontend 
to the database to model serving — detailed enough 
that another team could actually build from it 
• Results that include where the system fails, not just 
where it works 
2. WHAT OTHERS HAVE TRIED 
2.1 Rule-Based Systems and Their Limits 
For most of the history of digital fraud detection, the 
dominant tool was a ruleset. Transaction above a certain 
amount? Flag it. Location looks off? Flag it. 2 am and the 
user has never done this before? Flag it. Simple, auditable, 
cheap to run. 
Also, completely inadequate for anything resembling a 
modern fraud attack. Industry false positive rates hovered 
between 3% and 7% — annoying enough that users learned 
to dismiss alerts. Worse, updating a rule in response to a 
new fraud pattern took 72 to 96 hours to propagate across 
banking infrastructure (Bhattacharyya et al., 2011). The 
fraud ring had already moved on by then. You were always 
one step behind. 
Statistical anomaly detection was theoretically better. 
Bayesian networks and clustering approaches could surface 
unusual patterns without someone explicitly writing the 
rules. But they hit the fundamental wall of fraud data: fraud 
events are rare. Usually between 0.1% and 2% of total 
transactions. A lazy model that just predicts ‘not fraud’ for 
everything still achieves 98% accuracy. SMOTE and similar 
resampling methods help somewhat, but results are 
inconsistent across datasets. 
2.2 Machine Learning Gets Serious 
Supervised ML genuinely shifted things. Random Forests 
and XGBoost ensembles consistently top public fraud 
benchmarks — the best solutions on the IEEE-CIS Fraud 
Detection dataset cluster around AUC-ROC of 0.945. Better 
handling of class imbalance, better generalisation, and they 
can be retrained as fraud patterns shift. 
Problem is they still look at each transaction in isolation. 
Wang et al. (2019) was the paper that changed our thinking 
here — they applied a graph attentive network to Alipay 
transaction data and saw a 12.7% precision improvement 
over non-graph methods. The point is obvious in retrospect: 
a money mule network has a structural signature that no 
single-transaction model will ever see. That result is what 
pushed us toward building M6. 
2.3 The Indian Language Problem 
Phishing SMS detection is well studied — in English. 
Almeida et al. (2011) hit 97.5% accuracy with Naïve Bayes 
on the standard English SMS corpus. Great result. Mostly 
useless for India, where only 34% of the fraud messages we 
collected were even written in English. The rest was 
Devanagari Hindi, Roman-script Hinglish, Tamil, Telugu, 
Marathi, Kannada, Bengali, and a smattering of others. 
An English BERT model scores 71.3% on our corpus. We 
evaluated both IndicBERT (Kakwani et al., 2020) and MuRIL 
(Khanuja et al., 2021). MuRIL — google/muril-base-cased — 
won out for both M3 and M4 because of how it handles 
code-switching between Hindi and English (Hinglish is 
genuinely its strong suit), its broader coverage of 17 Indian 
languages, and the fact that it’s unrestricted on 
HuggingFace. Fine-tuned on our data it hits 97.1%. Using 
the same backbone for both M3 and M4 also means less 
storage overhead and faster cold starts — a practical win, 
not just a theoretical one. 
2.4 Detecting Fake Voices 
The ASVspoof 2021 challenge produced systems with equal 
error rates below 1% — in lab conditions. On actual 
telephonic audio, those numbers jump to 8–15%. 
Compressed, noisy, bandwidth-limited phone calls are a 
different problem from clean studio recordings. AASIST 
(Jung et al., 2022) and RawNet2 (Tak et al., 2021) both 
showed that end-to-end models trained directly on raw 
waveforms beat hand-crafted spectral features. That 
architecture is now what the better commercial APIs are 
built on. 
2.5 Finding Fraud Rings in Transaction 
Graphs 
Individual accounts in a money mule network look 
completely ordinary. The giveaway is structural — its in how 
they connect to each other, and how money flows between 
them. FdGars (Fan et al., 2020) and CARE-GNN (Dou et al., 
2020) both proved that GNNs can surface these structures 
in large payment graphs. We went with GraphSAGE 
(Hamilton et al., 2017) specifically because it’s inductive: it 
can score a UPI ID that didn’t exist during training by 
aggregating neighbourhood features. When your system 
sees thousands of brand-new accounts every day, that 
property isn’t a nice-to-have. It’s mandatory.

3.1 The Frontend: Quasar + Vite Quasar Framework v2 is a Vue.js-based framework that 
compiles to Android via Capacitor, iOS via Capacitor, and 
Progressive Web App from exactly one codebase. For a 
fraud prevention app that genuinely needs to work on a 
₹7,000 Redmi running on a patchy 2G connection in rural 
Bihar just as well as on a premium phone in Mumbai, 
compile-once-deploy-everywhere stops being a 
convenience and becomes an architectural requirement. 
Vite handles the build. HMR during development responds 
in under 50ms, which matters when you are iterating fast. 
Production builds go through Rollup, served via Nginx. 
Application state lives in four Pinia stores: auth manages 
JWT tokens and device fingerprints; fraud holds active risk 
scores and current alerts; transactions keeps the paginated 
payment history; and ws handles the WebSocket 
connection and routes incoming alert pushes to the right 
place. SMS reading, biometric authentication, background 
push notifications, and device fingerprinting all come 
through Capacitor plugins — the native layer that bridges 
Vue and the actual device hardware. 
3.2 The Backend: FastAPI + Celery 
FastAPI runs on a single Uvicorn-under-Gunicorn ASGI 
server handling both REST and persistent WebSocket 
connections. Anything heavy that doesn’t need to block an 
API response — batch ML scoring, NPCI blacklist sync, 
nightly graph retraining, deepfake audio processing — gets 
queued to Celery workers backed by Redis. That’s what 
keeps transaction scoring under 200ms at peak load. The 
heavy work happens asynchronously, and when its done, 
the result lands on the user’s device through WebSocket 
push. 
Three storage systems underpin everything. PostgreSQL via 
SQLAlchemy and Alembic for transactional records: users, 
transactions, fraud reports, model audit logs. Redis for sub-
millisecond cached fraud scores, blacklist lookups, and 
session state. Elasticsearch for full-text UPI ID search and 
fraud report retrieval. 
3.3 What Happens the Moment You Tap 
Pay 
On Android, Fraud Guard’s Accessibility Service catches the 
payment intent before it hits the UPI rail. On iOS, an App 
Extension does the same. The receiver’s UPI ID and amount 
get extracted immediately and sent to the scoring endpoint. 
Three things then run in parallel: a Redis cache check for 
recently scored UPI IDs (5-minute TTL), an NPCI blacklist 
lookup, and ML feature extraction. All three converge into 
a single aggregate risk score within 200ms of the user 
initiating the payment. That 200ms is the hard constraint 
the entire architecture is built around.

4.1 M1: The Core Transaction Scorer 
Every single UPI payment flows through M1 before the user 
sees a confirmation screen. It’s a soft-voting ensemble of 
Random Forest (300 trees, max depth 12) and XGBoost (200 
estimators, learning rate 0.05), equal weights on both. The 
combination isn’t arbitrary. Random Forest does a better 
job of smoothing out noise across high-variance transaction 
features. XGBoost handles class imbalance through its 
gradient boosting mechanics. Together they consistently 
beat either model alone by 6.4% on F1 — which is a 
meaningful margin when you are making decisions on 13 
billion transactions a month. 
The four features that carry the most weight: is_post_call 
(UPI payment within 5 minutes of an incoming call from an 
unknown number), remote_app_running (AnyDesk or 
TeamViewer active on the device — which is almost always 
a screen-sharing fraud), is_in_blacklist, and 
graph_risk_score from M6. Account age turns out to be 
surprisingly powerful — accounts under 7 days old have a 
fraud rate 3.2 times higher than older accounts. Simple but 
effective.

4.2 M2: UPI Identity Reputation 
M1 scores a transaction in context. M2 scores the identity 
on the other end, independent of any particular payment. 
This is what powers the ‘Verify UPI’ feature — the ability to 
check a UPI ID before you’ve committed to sending 
anything. LightGBM runs on account metadata: account 
age, total transaction volume, count of unique senders, 
fraud report rate per transaction, and how well the 
registered name actually matches the UPI handle. Scores 
stay live in Redis with TTLs tuned to activity level: 5 minutes 
for busy IDs, 30 for quieter ones. 
4.3 M3: Multilingual Phishing SMS 
Of all the fraud messages we collected, only 34% were in 
English. Hindi in Devanagari took 28%. Roman-script 
Hinglish — the way a lot of Indians type Hindi when using 
an English keyboard — was 24%. The remaining 14% was 
Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Kannada, Bengali, and others. A 
standard English BERT model scores 71.3% on this mix. 
That’s not good enough. Model choice here genuinely 
determines whether the thing works or not. 
We went with google/muril-base-cased as the M3 
backbone. MuRIL (Multilingual Representations for Indian 
Languages) was chosen over IndicBERT specifically because 
of how it handles Hinglish code-switching, its coverage of 
17 Indian languages rather than 11, and straightforward 
access on HuggingFace without licence friction. Fine-tuned 
on our fraud SMS corpus, it hits 97.1% accuracy. Beyond 
binary classification, the model flags five intent types: 
KYC_URGENCY, PRIZE_LOTTERY, 
AUTHORITY_IMPERSONATION, PAYMENT_REQUEST, and 
PHISHING_LINK — so users see a specific warning, not just 
‘suspicious’. 
4.4 M4: Voice Urgency Detection 
When Fraud Guard picks up a phone call, a two-stage 
pipeline runs quietly in the background. First, Whisper 
(OpenAI medium, 1.5 GB) transcribes the audio. It handles 
Hindi, English, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali and Marathi out of the 
box, without us having to fine-tune it for each language — 
important since we genuinely can’t predict what language a 
fraud call will arrive in. Then a fine-tuned classifier reads the 
transcript looking for five intents: URGENCY, 
IMPERSONATION, MONEY_REQUEST, SECRECY_DEMAND, 
and THREAT. Two or more firing at once flags the call. 
IMPERSONATION combined with MONEY_REQUEST alone 
reaches 94.7% precision for actual fraud calls in our 
validation set. 
M4 uses the same google/muril-base-cased backbone as 
M3. Deliberate choice. Both models benefit from MuRIL’s 
Hinglish and code-switching strengths, and sharing the base 
model cuts total storage footprint and cold-start time 
compared to loading two separate multilingual models. 
4.5 M5: Deepfake Audio Detection 
Primary deepfake detection runs through Resemble AI’s 
DETECT-2B API — a transformer model trained on ASVspoof 
2021 data supplemented with samples from 47 commercial 
TTS systems. It achieves 94–98% accuracy, audio is 
processed server-side and deleted immediately per the 
data agreement. For users who aren’t comfortable sending 
voice audio to a third party — which is a completely fair 
concern — we built an on-device alternative using a 
quantised AASIST model (Q8 INT8, 45 MB) for Android 
devices with more than 4 GB RAM. It gets 88.3% accuracy. 
Lower than the API, yes, but still a real and useful screening 
layer when privacy is the priority. The tradeoff is made 
explicit to the user before they opt in. 
4.6 M6: Fraud Network Graph 
Money mule networks don’t have a single obviously-bad 
account. The fraud is structural — its in how dozens of 
accounts are connected to each other and how money 
moves between them. M6 models the UPI ecosystem as a 
directed graph: nodes are UPI IDs, edges are transactions. 
GraphSAGE — two SAGEConv layers, 64 hidden dimensions, 
sigmoid output, class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss — 
runs as a nightly Celery task. Every morning it ingests the 
previous seven days of transactions, updates node features, 
and recomputes fraud probability scores. Scores cache in 
Redis for 24 hours and feed directly into M1 as the 
graph_risk_score feature. 
The specific reason we picked GraphSAGE over other GNNs: 
its inductive. It can produce a fraud score for a UPI ID that 
didn’t exist during training, by aggregating features from 
the accounts around it. That matters enormously in 
production, where thousands of brand-new UPI IDs appear 
every single day. 
5. THE DEEPFAKE VOICE PROBLEM 
5.1 Why This Is Different Now 
Three seconds. That’s genuinely all it takes to clone 
someone’s voice today. The source audio might be a short 
clip from Instagram, a WhatsApp forward that got passed 
around a family group, a comment someone left on 
YouTube. The fraudster doesn’t need any special 
equipment or technical skills. They need a browser, a paid 
subscription to one of several commercial TTS tools, and a 
phone. 
The clone that comes out sounds like family. In the moment 
— on a call with poor audio, in the middle of a described 
emergency — its nearly indistinguishable. And that’s the 
whole mechanism. The psychological pressure of hearing a 
familiar voice in apparent distress is powerful enough to 
short-circuit the usual scepticism. People don’t stop to 
verify. They send the money first. In Indian cases we 
reviewed from 2024, the average time between first 
contact and completed transfer was under ten minutes. 
The reason this is so difficult to detect at the system level is 
the exact same thing that makes it work at the human level. 
The voice sounds real. Blacklists, velocity checks, amount 
anomalies — none of them can see inside a phone call. You 
need an entirely different kind of signal. 
5.2 The Call-to-Transaction Correlation 
Algorithm 
The signal we found is temporal. In our dataset of 847 
confirmed vishing fraud cases, 91.3% involved a UPI 
payment within 10 minutes of an incoming call. For 
legitimate transactions, that proportion was 18.7%. The 
pattern isn’t subtle. 
The CTC algorithm operationalises this directly. It monitors 
for UPI payments initiated within a 5-minute window 
following an incoming call from a number that isn’t in the 
user’s contacts or Fraud Guard’s trusted list. When that 
correlation is detected, the is post call feature is set to True 
in M1, independently raising the fraud score regardless of 
what every other signal says.

a hard block. The user sees exactly why we are flagging it 
and makes their own decision. 
5.3 Catching Fake Numbers Before the Call 
Even Connects 
Most deepfake fraud calls come from VoIP numbers. 
Internet-routed calls that allow number spoofing and mask 
the real geographic origin. Fraud Guard integrates with 
Truecaller Spam Detection and Numverify to classify every 
incoming call as VoIP or genuine PSTN before the user even 
picks up. A warning banner shows on the incoming call 
screen. In our evaluation, that classification runs at 95.2% 
precision. No audio processing. No transcription. The 
warning appears before the phone has rung twice. That’s 
the fastest intervention point in the entire voice fraud 
chain, and the lightest one.

6.2 Does It Actually Scale? 
We ran a proper load test. 10,000 concurrent WebSocket 
connections. 1,000 transaction scoring requests per 
second. 4 FastAPI instances, 8 Celery workers. p95 latency 
held below 200ms the entire time. FastAPI instances are 
stateless, so adding more behind a load balancer requires 
zero application changes. Redis handles shared fraud score 
state and WebSocket pub/sub across all nodes. Celery 
workers autoscale on queue depth. The architecture does 
hold at the throughput that national deployment would 
actually require — we checked.

fraud transactions — a 0.64% fraud rate, which is very close 
to the actual real-world base rate. SMS classification was 
evaluated separately on 12,847 labelled messages from the 
community reporting feature.

7.3 Did Users Actually Act on the 
Warnings? 
False positive rate was 2.8% — below the 3–7% industry 
norm for rule-based systems. An app that flags legitimate 
transactions too often will get uninstalled, period. We were 
careful about this. 
When users saw a fraud warning, 67.3% chose to cancel 
rather than proceed. That’s the number we care most 
about. It tells us the warning design is actually working — 
people are reading it, understanding what it’s saying, and 
making real decisions based on it. Average time-to-action 
was 4.2 seconds. Not a reflexive dismiss. Not a panicked 
immediate cancel either. People are thinking. That was 
genuinely not guaranteed at the start of this project. 
8. WHAT WE LEARNED 
8.1 What Worked 
The six-model stack reaches 93.1% overall fraud detection 
at sub-200ms latency. The Call-to-Transaction Correlation 
algorithm is honestly the thing we are most satisfied with. 
It’s not technically complex. It’s just a clock watching for a 
pattern that is statistically overwhelming in real fraud data. 
The simplicity is kind of the point — it works because the 
underlying observation is genuinely reliable. 
The Quasar and Vite frontend deploys to Android, iOS, and 
PWA from one codebase. The FastAPI and Celery backend 
held up under national-scale load testing. False positive rate 
sits below industry norm. And 67.3% of users who received 
a warning actually cancelled their transaction. That last 
number — the behavioural one — matters more to us than 
any of the technical metrics. 
8.2 What Did Not Work as Well 
We want to be honest about this. 
The on-device deepfake model needs more than 4 GB RAM. 
A large share of India’s active smartphone market runs on 2 
GB devices. Those users fall back to the cloud API route, 
which means their voice audio leaves their device. That’s a 
real privacy compromise and we haven’t fully resolved it 
yet. 
The graph model retrains every night. A UPI ID created this 
morning for fraud won’t appear in M6’s risk scores until 
tomorrow morning. That’s a 24-hour blind spot for newly 
created accounts. And a sophisticated fraudster who knows 
about CTC can wait six or seven minutes between calling 
and asking for the transfer, which puts them just outside 
our correlation window. These aren’t theoretical risks. 
They’re real gaps in the current design. 
For users without any transaction history, cold-start 
performance is noticeably weaker. The system leans more 
heavily on community blacklists and UPI ID reputation 
signals, which are less personalised and sometimes slower 
to update. 
8.3 Where We Are Going Next 
• Real-time graph model updates driven by fraud 
reports — cutting the 24-hour batch cycle down 
toward near-real-time 
• Federated learning so the models can train across 
distributed user data without sensitive transaction 
records ever leaving individual devices 
• A lightweight on-device fraud model under 50 MB that 
runs on 2 GB RAM budget smartphones — the ones 
most Indian users actually have 
• Deepfake detection extended to video calls on 
WhatsApp and Zoom, using facial landmark 
inconsistency detection alongside audio analysis 
• A proper longitudinal study: do people who receive 
fraud warnings actually change how they behave over 
time, or do they just habituate and start ignoring 
alerts? 
 
“The goal is not smarter fraud detection. The goal is 
fraud detection that is invisible to honest users and 
impossible for fraudsters to outrun.”
